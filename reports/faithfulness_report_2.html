Quantifying the Faithfulness of Text Classification Explanations

Second experiment: four attribution methods, three evaluation budgets, 300 test documents per dataset · companion to the first report, which carries the literature review · seed 42

Abstract

This round re-runs the deletion faithfulness benchmark on stronger foundations: 4000 document checkpoints with a proper 70/15/15 train, validation, test split (test accuracy 0.89 to 0.96), explanation evaluated exclusively on the test split, 300 stratified documents of its 600 per dataset, three times round one. The roster grows to four methods, SHAP, LIME, leave one word out occlusion, and an explicit gaussian random baseline that replaces the shuffled controls, and the budget axis grows to three points, 50, 200 and 500 model evaluations for SHAP and LIME. All results are reported as a percentage of the model's original predicted class confidence.

Every real method beats the random baseline decisively, SHAP in 54 of 54 Holm corrected conditions, LIME in 54, occlusion in 52, and none ever loses to it, so the metric separates knowledge from noise. Head to head at 500 evaluations, 25 conditions favor SHAP and 6 favor LIME of 31 significant; SHAP costs 7.8 seconds per explained document on average against LIME's 18.7. Across every method, direction and chunked condition, absolute_sum never loses a Holm corrected comparison against the other two combinations; the budget ladders are resolved in the conclusion.

1. Experimental setup

Three binary text classification datasets, one shared pipeline: frozen e5-small-v2 embeddings, a single hidden layer MLP head, 4000 stratified documents per dataset split 70/15/15. The head's early stopping uses an internal slice of the training set, so the validation split stays clean and the test split is used for exactly one thing, explanation evaluation. All explained documents are drawn from the test split, 300 of 600, stratified on the label.

Four attribution methods produce one signed value per whitespace word: SHAP (partition explainer, budget capped), LIME (ridge surrogate over word dropouts, budget exact), occlusion (leave one word out, cost fixed at one prediction per word) and random, an independent standard gaussian draw per word that costs no predictions and replaces round one's per method shuffled controls. SHAP and LIME run at 50, 200 and 500 evaluations; cross method comparisons use the 500 budget. Faithfulness is measured exactly as in round one: cut chunks of 1, 4 or 8 words ranked by the attribution under three combinations, at thresholds of 20 to 80 percent of the document, in both directions, and average the threshold results into one score per document, expressed relative to the model's original confidence.

2. Definitions

Write f(s) for the model's probability of the predicted class on text s, fixed from the full text. With cut thresholds t1 < … < tn (20, 40, 60, 80), s∕t− the text with chunks below the t-th percentile of its own chunk importances removed, and s∕t+ its most important mirror:

sufficiency(s) = (1/n) Σi |f(s) − f(s∕ti−)| / f(s)

comprehensiveness(s) = (1/n) Σi |f(s) − f(s∕ti+)| / f(s)

Both are shares of the model's original confidence, in [0, 1], reported in percent. Sufficiency is better when lower, comprehensiveness when higher. The first report derives these step by step with a worked example; this one uses them directly.

4. Conclusion

4.1 Does the metric make sense? The random attribution test

A faithfulness metric is meaningful only if knowing something beats knowing nothing. It does, without exception: against the gaussian random baseline, SHAP wins 54, LIME 54 and occlusion 52 of 54 Holm corrected conditions each, and no method loses a single condition to random in either direction. The margins are large as well as significant, mean advantages of 10.1, 8.3 and 6.5 percentage points of confidence respectively. The metric separates informed rankings from noise; what remains is separating informed rankings from each other.

4.2 SHAP or LIME?

At the shared 500 budget the verdict is no longer close: of 54 conditions, 31 separate the methods after Holm correction, 25 favoring SHAP and 6 favoring LIME, with a mean advantage of +1.9 percentage points of confidence toward SHAP. LIME's wins cluster where round one already found them, word level sufficiency on the news topic task, whose signal concentrates in a few entity terms; SHAP takes comprehensiveness nearly everywhere and sufficiency on both sentiment tasks. On cost the gap compounds: SHAP explains a document in 7.8 seconds on average against LIME's 18.7 at the same budget, a factor of 2.4, because SHAP's masker collapses removed spans while LIME's perturbed texts stay near full length. More faithful on most conditions and less than half the price: for this pipeline the head to head goes to SHAP.

4.3 Which evaluation budget is enough?

These are rather short texts, medians of 157, 103 and 38 words, so the three budgets bracket the word counts: 50 evaluations sits below every median, 500 above all of them. The answer differs sharply by method. LIME needs budget: its full arm beats the 50 arm in 48 of 54 conditions, with both intermediate rungs significant (12 and 13 conditions), consistent with a ridge surrogate that is underdetermined until its sample count clears the word count. SHAP is nearly budget saturated on these texts: 500 beats 200 in only 8 of 54 conditions, the gains concentrating in comprehensiveness while sufficiency is flat, and the end to end comparison even reverses in 4 word level sufficiency conditions where the coarse 50 evaluation partition is marginally better. The practical recommendation for texts of this length: SHAP at 200 evaluations is already a sound operating point and 50 is usable, while LIME should not be run below 200 and prefers 500; at the smallest budget SHAP is the only sensible choice of the two.

4.4 Which chunk combination?

Judged per method on the chunked sizes, where the combinations actually differ (Tables 6 and 7), the answer is unusually clean: absolute_sum is the best mean for both methods in both directions, and across all 48 Holm corrected pairwise conditions involving it, it wins 24 and loses none. The margins are largest in comprehensiveness, over one and a half percentage points of confidence against either alternative, exactly where a span's ability to cancel opposing words matters most. The other two combinations are statistically indistinguishable from each other. This is the outcome theory predicts for additive attributions, the signed sum of a chunk is its expected output shift, and it holds empirically for LIME's non additive weights as well: whichever of SHAP or LIME is used, absolute_sum is the combination to choose, and the ranking of methods reported above is unchanged under either alternative.
